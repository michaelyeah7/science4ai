Science for AI

A model can read every paper in a field and still not run one experiment in it.

The published record keeps the answer and drops what the answer rests on. We are building the missing half from both ends: compiling published methods into exercises an agent can execute and be scored on, and capturing, at the bench, the reasoning behind an experiment before its result is known.

the gap

The record keeps the answer

A result is published once it works. The conditions it worked under, the controls that made it believable, the variants that did nothing and the reason anyone ran the experiment are not part of what gets published.1 A human reader fills those in from experience, or writes to the author. A model has neither option.

from the literature

A procedure can keep its formula and lose the condition that makes the formula appropriate

Take a careful methods paper, compile it into an exercise that can actually be run, then score what survived against the source. The pattern is consistent: nearly everything the compiled version says is supported by the paper, while a substantial part of what the paper requires in order to use the method correctly has disappeared.

What goes missing is rarely the equation. It is the condition the equation holds under, the range where an approximation is valid, the unit. The result reads well. That is the problem: it reads well enough to be reused where it does not apply.

from the laboratory

An observation can be true, publishable and useless for deciding anything

A reporter can shift a long way at baseline while the response you were trying to measure does not move at all. The variant changed the signal is true, and it is the sentence that reaches a paper. It is not the sentence the laboratory needs, because it says nothing about whether the assay could have supported a conclusion in the first place.

The useful next experiment is not another variant. It is the control that decides whether the question was asked at all — and no part of that decision survives into the published record.

It has been solved once

Structure prediction did not begin with a model. It began with fifty years of a single open archive, one format, journals that required deposition, and a blind test that scored predictions against structures nobody had seen.23 The model was the last step and the fastest. The archive is the part that took fifty years.

Most experimental science has neither the archive nor the test. Results sit in supplementary spreadsheets and instrument exports, in formats that change from lab to lab, and the reasoning that produced them is not written down anywhere.

what we build

Two halves of one record

The literature and the laboratory lose the same thing. So we work on both ends, and make them emit the same unit.

compile

A published method becomes an exercise that can be scored

The assumptions and conditions the method holds under, the question to answer, the tools and operations it needs, the evidence actually available, a check that runs as code, and the terms under which the source may be used. An agent works the exercise and is scored by running the check — not by another model's opinion of its prose.

What the agent learns is kept as a reusable instruction and a program it can run again. Its weights never change. What improves is the set of procedures it can retrieve, and every improvement had to pass a check to be kept.

Compiling well is itself measurable: score the output against its source for how much of what it says is supported, and how much of what the source requires it retained. Those are different skills, and the second is the harder one.

capture

An experiment becomes a commitment and its settlement

Before the run: the objective and its constraints, the panel with every member's role, the controls, and a written prediction with a confidence attached. After the run: the measurements, a verdict, and the next action. The prediction is frozen rather than rewritten, and the outcome is appended to it.4

Three judgments stay apart that are usually collapsed into one: whether the assay was valid, whether the hypothesis was supported, and whether the outcome fell inside any branch that was anticipated. An invalid assay does not produce a negative result. A valid surprise does not have to be forced into a branch someone wrote down in advance.

This pays for itself before any model is trained. A lab that writes its predictions down first can count how often it was right, see which assumption a whole round was resting on, and tell a refuted idea from an experiment that never tested it.

Both halves emit the same unit

A claim, the evidence under it, the conditions it holds within, and a check that can be run against it. That is what a model can learn from, and what a person can audit. Everything else is commentary — useful to read, impossible to verify.

evaluation

What would count as working

Three claims get confused with one another constantly. We keep them apart, because each needs its own evidence and the first does not imply the second.

  1. Claim 1

    The experiment was a good choice

    Measured against expert selection and simple baselines, at a matched experimental budget, on cycles that were actually run. A control can be the right answer: an experiment that qualifies the assay beats one that adds a variant nobody can interpret.

  2. Claim 2

    Learning from these records makes later experiments better

    A frozen baseline, a defined update, and a measured gain on held-out campaigns, scaffolds and dates.5 A better fit to records already collected is not evidence of this and is easy to mistake for it.

  3. Claim 3

    Changing how the system learns improved the learning

    A separate question again, tested against the current system before anything replaces it. A system may propose a change to its own process; it does not get to grade that change, or to decide it has earned more authority.

What is not established

The architecture is specified, and parts of it are built and measured. End-to-end autonomous operation, improved proteins, and a demonstrated benefit from training on process records are not among them.

We say so on purpose. A program that cannot state what it has not shown cannot be checked either — and a record layer whose whole point is checkability should not begin by exempting itself.

where it runs

data points is the most that one of them, the DREAM Cloud Lab, plans to release. It also plans to build and measure more than 300,000 proteins.7

Automated laboratories are about to multiply the volume of experimental data by a large factor. Whether records at that scale are something a model can learn from is settled by what gets captured while the runs are happening.

Afterwards, it cannot be reconstructed. A prediction that was never written down cannot be recovered from the measurement it was meant to anticipate, and a condition nobody recorded is not in the file.

about

Who is behind this

Science4AI is maintained by Guo Ye, a data scientist at the DREAM Cloud Lab in Northwestern University's Center for Synthetic Biology, one of the 20 nodes in the NSF Programmable Cloud Laboratories Test Bed. It grows out of the day-to-day work of making an automated protein-engineering lab's output usable for training AI, and out of two manuscripts in preparation on the two halves described above.

This is a working document and it will change. If something here is wrong, missing or out of date, open an issue or propose an edit on GitHub. A related project, Autonomous Lab Bench, proposes a cross-laboratory benchmark for scientific agents.
