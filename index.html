A paper reports three wells. A model needs all ninety-six.

AI for science asks what models can do for a laboratory. Science for AI asks the reverse: what does a laboratory have to record for a model to learn from it? This is a working guide to that question.

The dataset came first

The best-known success of AI in science rested on a dataset before it rested on a model. The Protein Data Bank opened in 1971 with seven structures. Over the next five decades structural biologists deposited their results in that one open archive, in a shared format, and journals came to require it.1 By the time AlphaFold 2 was trained, the archive held about 170,000 structures, and the field had been running a blind test since 1994 in which predictions are scored against structures nobody has yet seen.2

1 Berman et al., J. Comput. Aided Mol. Des. (2014).

2 DeepMind (2020); Jumper et al., Nature (2021).

Most experimental science has neither the archive nor the test. Results sit in supplementary spreadsheets, instrument exports and notebooks, in formats that change from lab to lab. Experiments that did not work are rarely written down in a form anyone else can use. The reasons an experiment was run at all, which is most of what a scientist knows, are not recorded anywhere.

Automated laboratories are about to multiply the volume. In July 2026 the US National Science Foundation committed $380 million to 20 programmable cloud laboratories, with shared standards for protocols, metadata, data and AI as part of the brief.4 One of them, the DREAM Cloud Lab at Northwestern, plans to build and measure more than 300,000 proteins and release up to 30 million data points.5 Whether records like these are something a model can learn from is decided by what gets captured at the bench, and it is decided now.

4 NSF announcement, 22 July 2026.

5 Northwestern Engineering, July 2026.

Eight questions a model asks of a dataset

A reader who is confused can write to the first author. A model cannot.

Whatever a model needs has to be in the record already. These eight questions are a way to check a dataset before release. Each one names the part of the record that answers it.

identity

What exactly was measured?

Names drift; identifiers do not. Give the full sequence, structure or composition, not a nickname. Say which numbering convention you are using. Put a unit on every quantity.

Capture sequences or structure identifiers, units, ontology terms, the numbering frame.

A scan of genomics journals found gene names silently converted to dates by spreadsheet software in about a fifth of papers with supplementary Excel gene lists.6

conditions

Under what conditions?

The same sample gives a different number at another temperature, in another buffer, on another instrument. Conditions are what let a model explain variance instead of memorizing it.

Capture protocol version, instrument and settings, reagent lots, temperature, timing, operator or agent.

Automated experimentation helps here. It lets a lab capture metadata at every step of a workflow, which makes the experiment easier to reproduce and the data easier to learn from.7

uncertainty

How sure are you?

A value without its spread teaches false confidence. Report replicates one by one, not only their mean. Keep each QC verdict together with the rule it was judged against. State the floor and ceiling of the assay.

Capture replicate-level values, QC pass or fail with the criterion, detection limits, calibration.

negatives

What did not work?

The literature is a record of successes, so a model trained on it has never seen failure. A dataset that keeps only the hits teaches a model where the peaks are and nothing about the ground between them.

Capture every sample that was run, including nulls, inactive variants and failed runs, flagged and not deleted.

One group transcribed the failed syntheses from its own notebooks and trained on them with the successes. The model’s recommended reactions worked 89% of the time; the chemists’ own judgment, 78%.8

comparability

Can this be compared with anything else?

Data from two plates, two weeks or two labs can be merged only if something ties them together. Shared controls and a reference sample carried through every run let a model learn the science and not the batch.

Capture controls on every plate, anchor samples across rounds, calibration to standard units, batch identifiers.

splits

Is the test set really unseen?

Scientific data is full of near-duplicates: homologous proteins, one compound in two assays, a time series cut at random. Ship the split with the data, and say what it is meant to hold out.

Capture train and test assignment, the grouping rule (by family, scaffold, date or lab), known overlaps with public sets.

A survey of machine-learning-based science found data leakage affecting at least 294 papers across 17 fields.9

rights

Am I allowed to learn from it?

A dataset with no license, or with terms that are silent on model training, is not ready however clean it is. Neither is one whose consent or embargo status nobody can state.

Capture license, consent and embargo status, a persistent identifier, how to cite.

A clear, accessible usage license is one of the FAIR principles.10

rationale

Why was this experiment run?

Every design choice encodes knowledge: what was expected, what it rested on, what was ruled out. None of it survives in a results table. It is the part of the record nobody keeps, and the subject of the next section.

Capture the question, the choice made, the alternatives rejected, a prediction written down before the result.

What happened, and why it was done

Laboratory software answers one question well: what happened. Plate, well, sample, condition, value. It does not hold the answer to the question a scientist would ask first: why does this well exist?

The first layer can be captured by instruments, automatically. The second has to be written down by whoever made the decision, a scientist or an agent, before the result is known. Kept together, they turn an archive into something a model can be scored against: a prediction, an outcome and a verdict for every choice.

Our working hypothesis is that the second layer is what separates a model that predicts assay results from one that can plan an experiment. It is a hypothesis, not a finding. The nearest evidence comes from surgery, where one group stopped labeling more video frames and instead harvested the reasoning in surgeons’ narrated teaching videos; models trained on it outperformed larger general-purpose models on questions about intent and rationale.11

11 Perez, Rau et al., arXiv (2026).

It also pays for itself before any model is trained. A lab that writes its predictions down first can count how often it was right, see which assumptions a whole round of designs was resting on, and tell a refuted idea from an experiment that never tested it.

Five levels of readiness

Guidance on data usually stops once a dataset can be found and is well described. That is necessary, and a model needs more. This ladder is our working synthesis. The lower rungs map onto standards that already exist; the top rung is where we think the open problem is.

  1. Level 0

    Stored

    Files exist. What they mean lives in filenames and in someone’s memory.

    Usable by the person who made it.

  2. Level 1

    Shared

    Persistent identifiers, standard formats, descriptive metadata and a license: the FAIR principles.10

    Usable by another scientist who goes looking.

  3. Level 2

    Loadable

    A datasheet explains how the data was collected and what it should not be used for.12 Machine-readable metadata such as Croissant lets ML tools load it without hand-written parsing.13

    Usable by a training pipeline.

  4. Level 3

    Learnable

    Conditions, uncertainty, negatives, comparability, splits and rights are all in the release, in the spirit of criteria such as NIH Bridge2AI’s seven dimensions of AI-readiness.14

    Usable by a model that predicts outcomes.

  5. Level 4

    Reasoned Open problem

    The record also carries the decisions: why each experiment was run, what was predicted, and how the result changed the next one.

    Usable by a model that plans experiments.

Who is behind this

Science4AI is maintained by Guo Ye, a data scientist at the DREAM Cloud Lab in Northwestern University’s Center for Synthetic Biology, one of the 20 nodes in the NSF Programmable Cloud Laboratories Test Bed. It grows out of the day-to-day work of making an automated protein-engineering lab’s output usable for training AI.

This is a working document and it will change. If something here is wrong, missing or out of date, open an issue or propose an edit on GitHub. A related project, Autonomous Lab Bench, proposes a cross-laboratory benchmark for scientific agents.
